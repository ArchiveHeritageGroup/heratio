# Digitising the Un-Digitisable

### Heritage is not flat. And the material a scanner cannot touch is usually the first to be lost, because nobody scoped it.

A digitisation programme is written, budgeted and approved. It covers the paper: files, registers, correspondence, photographs. It gets underway, and it is going well.

Down the corridor, in the same building, sit the things the programme quietly ignored. A shelf of reel-to-reel tapes. A box of VHS cassettes holding the only recordings of interviews with people now dead. A few cans of 16mm film. A carved mask, a beaded garment, a drawer of coins. None of them fit under a scanner, so none of them were scoped. And of everything the institution holds, these are the items with the least time left.

This is the blind spot in most digitisation thinking. We treat digitisation as one problem with one method, when it is really several, each with its own technique, its own standard, and, crucially, its own clock.

## The material with a clock

Paper is patient. A minute book from 1953 will still be readable in 2043; you can digitise it next year or the year after and lose nothing. Magnetic and film media are neither patient nor forgiving.

Magnetic tape decays chemically. The binder that holds the oxide to the backing absorbs moisture and breaks down, the tape turns sticky and sheds its signal, and the recording degrades a little more every year. Acetate film suffers vinegar syndrome, an acidic decay that, once it starts, accelerates on its own and consumes the reel. At the same time the machines needed to play these formats are vanishing. Nobody manufactures open-reel decks, U-matic or Betacam recorders, or DAT machines any more. The spare parts are a finite pool, and the engineers who can align a replay head correctly are retiring.

The International Association of Sound and Audiovisual Archives is blunt about what this means: audiovisual transfer is a rescue operation, not routine reformatting, and the window is closing carrier by carrier and machine by machine. You are not deciding when to digitise these tapes. You are deciding whether you will still be able to.

## One pass, done right

There is a second, sharper reason A/V is unlike paper. A fragile tape may survive only one more play. The very act of transferring it can be the thing that destroys it. There is often no second attempt.

So it has to be right the first time: the correct machine, correctly calibrated for speed, azimuth and equalisation, capturing the full signal flat, at the full specification, in a single pass. And captured honestly, with no noise reduction, no picture enhancement, no cleaning up on the master. The job is to preserve the signal the carrier holds as completely as possible, so a future restorer can start from the truth rather than from someone's 2026 idea of an improvement. Audio goes to uncompressed WAV or Broadcast Wave; video to a lossless format such as FFV1 in Matroska, captured through a time-base corrector; film is scanned frame by frame at 2K or 4K. None of this is exotic. FADGI and the IASA technical guidelines lay it out in detail. It is simply different work, done by different people, against a different deadline.

## The things that are not flat at all

Then there are the objects. A carved mask, a coin, a ceramic pot, a beaded garment. Photograph one and you have flattened away most of what matters: the depth of the carving, the true dimensions, the way light moves across a glazed or beaded surface. For an object, the record is not an image. It is geometry, surface colour and texture, and real-world scale, together.

Capturing that is a solved problem too, with its own toolkit: photogrammetry from many overlapping photographs, structured-light or laser scanning for precise geometry, reflectance transformation imaging for surface detail on coins and inscriptions. Which method you use depends on the object. What does not change is the discipline. A colour chart and a physical scale bar have to be in the capture every time, or the model is device-dependent and cannot be measured against the real thing. The source photographs from a photogrammetry run are themselves part of the preservation record, not disposable working files. And the documentation follows recognised cultural-heritage standards, CARARE and the 3D-ICONS guidelines, with CIDOC-CRM for the object's context, so the result is interoperable rather than a lonely file in a proprietary format.

## The triage most programmes get backwards

Put those two facts side by side and the usual order of work is exactly wrong. The paper, which can wait a decade, gets done first because it is easy and visible and fits the scanner the institution already owns. The audiovisual and the objects, which cannot wait, get deferred to a later phase that often never comes, or get handed to whoever will do them cheapest and come back as lossy files with the picture enhanced and no scale reference in sight.

The governance failure is not technical. The standards exist, the methods exist, the tools exist. The failure is that the digitisation specification was written for flat paper, the fragile and time-critical material was never scoped into it, and so the collection with the shortest clock is the last thing anyone attends to.

## The point

The scanner was never the hard part. Neither is the camera, or the 3D rig. The hard part is recognising that digitisation is not one problem. It is audio, video, film, objects and text, each with its own method, its own standard, and its own deadline. Treating them as one thing means quietly accepting the loss of the ones that could not wait.

For flat paper, the decision you defer costs you a year. For a forty-year-old tape, the decision you defer is the recording you will never hear again. Scope the material with a clock first. Everything else can wait. That cannot.
