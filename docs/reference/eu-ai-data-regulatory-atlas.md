# EU AI and data regulatory atlas (HARNESS D1.1) and what it means for Heratio

**Summary.** HARNESS D1.1 is an EU-funded systematic mapping of the nine regulatory instruments now governing AI and data in the European Union, published open access under CC-BY in June 2026. Its central finding is that the apparent fragmentation of that landscape hides a shared regulatory logic, but that coherence weakens exactly where the instruments intersect. For the AHG it is useful in three ways: it is the strongest available citation for keeping compliance regimes pluggable rather than baked into the core, it can be quoted freely in the digital-sovereignty work, and what it leaves out is arguably more valuable than what it covers. Nine instruments regulate how AI and data are processed and placed on the market. None of them asks whether the record an AI system grounds itself on is authentic, or who vouches for it.

## The document

HARNESS D1.1, Atlas of AI and Data Regulatory Frameworks, version 1. Claudia Silva Battagin, Trinity College Dublin, published 26 June 2026. Researchers Gabriela Kurteva and Kimmy Shah; supervisor Maria Grazia Porcedda. DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20936479, Creative Commons Attribution 4.0, indexed in OpenAIRE. Produced under the European Commission's HARNESS project, Harnessing AI and Data-Intensive Technologies, grant 101169409.

It is a project deliverable, not a peer-reviewed article. Cite it as a mapping exercise and a well-sourced survey, not as an authority on findings.

## What it maps

The instruments covered are the AI Act, GDPR, Data Governance Act, Data Act, Digital Services Act, Digital Markets Act, Cyber Resilience Act, the NIS2 Directive and related legislation. Rather than describing each in turn, the analysis asks a harder question across all of them: do they agree on who the regulated actors are, what obligations attach to them, and how deterrence works.

The answers, in short. There is a shared regulatory logic underneath what looks from outside like a fragmented field. Coherence varies depending on which dimension you test, and it degrades at the points where two instruments overlap. Technical standards carry a great deal of the implementation weight, which places real regulatory power with standards bodies. And the framework may need reassessment as the technology moves, which is the deliverable's own hedge and should be read as one.

## Why it matters here

**It supports the pluggable-compliance architecture.** Heratio's core is jurisdiction-neutral and country-specific regimes ship as modules alongside it. That is a design position that occasionally needs defending, usually to someone who wants GDPR or POPIA logic wired straight into the core because it is the only regime they have to satisfy. This deliverable is an EU-funded document establishing that the EU alone is nine overlapping instruments whose seams are where the difficulty concentrates. A core that hard-codes any single regime inherits that instability. See `feedback_international_positioning` for the underlying rule.

**It is freely citable in the sovereignty work.** CC-BY means it can be quoted directly in the digital-sovereignty paper and in EU-facing capability documents with attribution and no permission friction. Relevant to `isj-digital-sovereignty-paper-angle`.

**Its omission is the AHG's opening.** This is the part worth carrying forward. Nine instruments govern processing, market conduct, risk classification and cybersecurity. Not one of them addresses provenance: whether the record a system relies on is what it claims to be, whether its custody chain holds, whether anyone is accountable for vouching for it. Regulated AI is increasingly obliged to ground and cite its sources, and the regulation says nothing about the trustworthiness of the thing being cited. That gap is the trustworthy-memory thesis stated by omission, which is a stronger way to open the argument than asserting it unaided. See `ahg-strategic-angle-trustworthy-memory` and `trust-api-prototype-spec`.

## Cautions when using it

It says nothing about cultural heritage, archives, libraries or museums. Do not cite it as though it addressed the GLAM sector; its subject is the general AI and data economy.

It is version 1 of a live project deliverable, so check for a later version before citing in anything durable.

The reassessment point is a hedge rather than a finding, and quoting it as a conclusion would overstate what the document supports.

Being EU-scoped, it is evidence about one market. It does not tell us anything directly about the SADC, UK, Australian or North American regimes, and should not be generalised into claims about them.
